\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Semantic segmentation assigns a label to every pixel of an image, and training
normally assumes that each image has one correct mask. In medical imaging that
assumption does not hold. Several qualified experts asked to outline the same
lesion will produce visibly different masks, and on the LIDC-IDRI lung-nodule
dataset they sometimes disagree about whether a lesion is present at all. A
conventional U-Net trained on one expert's mask, or on the average of several,
returns a single confident segmentation and discards this information precisely
where it is most clinically relevant.

The Probabilistic U-Net addresses this by combining a U-Net with a conditional
variational autoencoder. A prior network maps the image $x$ to a distribution
$p(z\mid x)$ over a low-dimensional latent space; each draw of $z$ is decoded
into a different plausible segmentation. The samples are coherent whole masks
rather than independent per-pixel probabilities, which is the paper's central
design argument: a pixel-wise probability map cannot express which pixels vary
\emph{together}, and so cannot represent two distinct but internally consistent
interpretations of the same image. Training minimises the negative evidence lower
bound, in which a posterior $q(z\mid x,Y_g)$ additionally observes one randomly
chosen expert mask $Y_g$,
\[
  L_{\text{PU-Net}}
  = \underbrace{\mathrm{E}_{z\sim q(z\mid x,Y_g)}\!\left[-\log p(Y_g\mid x,z)\right]}
      _{\text{reconstruction (cross-entropy, summed over pixels)}}
    \;+\; \beta\,\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q(z\mid x,Y_g)\,\|\,p(z\mid x)\right)}
      _{\text{pulls the prior towards the posterior}} ,
\]
so that at test time sampling from the prior alone reproduces the variability
seen across annotators. The balance between the two terms is consequential:
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports how an incorrect reduction of the
reconstruction term collapses the posterior onto the prior and removes the
model's stochasticity entirely.

The resulting distribution of segmentations is scored with the generalized energy
distance. Writing $S,S'$ for independent model samples and $Y,Y'$ for independent
grader masks,
\[
  D^2_{\mathrm{GED}}
  = 2\,\mathrm{E}\!\left[d(S,Y)\right]
    - \underbrace{\mathrm{E}\!\left[d(S,S')\right]}_{\text{sample diversity}}
    - \mathrm{E}\!\left[d(Y,Y')\right],
  \qquad d = 1-\mathrm{IoU},
\]
where $\mathrm{IoU}(A,B)=|A\cap B|/|A\cup B|$ is the intersection over union of
two binary masks.

We chose this paper because LIDC-IDRI provides four independent expert masks per
image, which makes inter-rater disagreement directly measurable rather than
merely assumed --- a rare property among segmentation datasets. It also exposes a
specific gap. Every term of $D^2_{\mathrm{GED}}$ is a scalar:
$\mathrm{E}[d(S,S')]$ measures how \emph{much} the model's samples vary and
$\mathrm{E}[d(Y,Y')]$ how much the graders vary, but nothing in the metric asks
\emph{where} in the image that variation falls. A model could therefore produce
the right amount of variability in entirely the wrong places and score just as
well as one that does not. Answering the ``where'' question requires the two
spatial maps that lie behind those scalars, and comparing them is what we add.

\section{Proposed extension}\label{sec:ext}

Three quantities must be kept distinct. The first, $\Dgt$, is \emph{human}
disagreement, computed from the four masks alone. With
$P_i=\frac{1}{G}\sum_g Y_i^{(g)}$ the per-pixel mean over $G=4$ graders, $\Dgt$
is its normalised binary entropy,
\[
  \Dgt_i=-\frac{P_i\log(P_i+\epsilon)+(1-P_i)\log(1-P_i+\epsilon)}{\log 2}\in[0,1],
\]
which is $0$ wherever all four graders agree and $1$ where they split two against
two. The second, $\hat D$, is our model's \emph{prediction} of $\Dgt$. The third,
$U$, is \emph{model} uncertainty: we draw $K$ latents from the prior, decode each
into a foreground-probability map $Q^{(k)}$, average them into
$\bar Q_i=\frac{1}{K}\sum_k Q_i^{(k)}$, and apply the same functional,
\[
  U_i=-\frac{\bar Q_i\log(\bar Q_i+\epsilon)+(1-\bar Q_i)\log(1-\bar Q_i+\epsilon)}{\log 2}.
\]
Because $U$ and $\Dgt$ differ only in what is averaged --- model samples in one
case, grader votes in the other --- any discrepancy between them is a discrepancy
of location on a common $[0,1]$ scale, which is what makes $|U-\Dgt|$ a
meaningful quantity to minimise.

Our extension adds two components, both deliberately minimal. The
\textbf{disagreement head} is a single $1\times1$ convolution followed by a
sigmoid, applied to the last U-Net feature map and tapped \emph{before} the
latent $z$ is injected, so that $\hat D$ is a function of the image alone; it is
trained with $L_D=\mathrm{MSE}(\hat D,\Dgt)$. The \textbf{alignment loss}
$L_{\text{align}}=\frac{1}{HW}\sum_i|U_i-\Dgt_i|$ penalises predictive diversity
that lands anywhere other than where the graders differ. The full objective is
$L=L_{\text{PU-Net}}+\lambda_D L_D+\lambda_A L_{\text{align}}$, and
Figure~\ref{fig:model} shows both additions in place.

\input{figure_model}

It is worth being precise about how these additions relate to the original
design. $\Dgt$ and $U$ are per-pixel marginals: projections of the joint
distribution over masks that the paper deliberately models. We do not substitute
one object for the other. The conditional VAE continues to own the joint --- the
KL term keeps the prior broad and the decoded samples internally coherent --- and
$L_{\text{align}}$ constrains only where the marginal variance is located. $U$ is
computed from the $K$ decoded samples, so it is a readout of that distribution
rather than a parallel pixel-wise model. Four masks determine the per-pixel
Bernoulli exactly, so that with $G=4$ the map $\Dgt$ takes only the values
$\{0,\,0.81,\,1\}$, but they say very little about the joint; a marginal
constraint is close to all that four annotations can support. The head $\hat D$
is genuinely pixel-wise, a $1\times1$ convolution on image features with no
sample involved, and functions as an auxiliary output alongside the sample-based
segmentation rather than as a replacement for it.

This yields two testable claims. \textbf{Q1:} where annotators will disagree is
predictable from the image alone, so that $\hat D$ approximates $\Dgt$ and
outperforms a trivial predictor that merely marks lesion boundaries.
\textbf{Q2:} adding $L_{\text{align}}$ increases the agreement between $U$ and
$\Dgt$ relative to the baseline without degrading segmentation quality or the
energy distance.

We considered two alternatives. Conditioning the prior on disagreement,
$p(z\mid x,\hat D)$, was rejected because $\hat D$ is a deterministic function of
$x$ and therefore contributes no new information while confounding the ablation.
Per-rater output heads or annotator confusion matrices~\cite{zhang2020} were
rejected because the LIDC release does not identify which radiologist produced
which mask and the four raters differ from scan to scan, leaving a per-rater
parameterisation nothing stable to learn.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Model.} We reimplemented the Probabilistic U-Net in PyTorch, the
official release being an older TensorFlow codebase that does not run on current
Colab. The architecture follows the original: a U-Net backbone with 32 base
channels and four down- and up-sampling stages, a prior encoder, a posterior
encoder, a six-dimensional latent, and \texttt{fcomb}-style injection of $z$
into the final $1\times1$ convolution stack. Our additions live in a subclass, so
the unmodified baseline remains runnable and every arm shares one code path.

\paragraph{Data.} We use the LIDC-IDRI 2D crops from DeepMind's preprocessed
release (CC BY 3.0), published with the Hierarchical Probabilistic
U-Net~\cite{kohl2019}, whose preprocessing follows Appendix H.1 of the original
paper. The splits contain 8843 training, 1993 validation and 1980 test images
from 530, 111 and 103 patients respectively; images are $180\times180$ 8-bit
grayscale with four masks each. We take a random $128\times128$ crop, scale
images to $[0,1]$ and binarise the masks. These counts are up to 0.6\% below the
8882 / 1996 / 1992 reported in the paper, because the authors cleaned the dataset
after publication and the 2018 snapshot was never released; the numbers here
refer to the HPU-Net release. Roughly 65\% of training crops contain at least one
empty mask, reflecting disagreement about lesion \emph{existence} rather than
boundary placement, which is why the empty-mask convention of
Section~\ref{sec:results} matters.

\paragraph{Training.} The objective, optimiser and schedule are taken unchanged
from the original paper: the reconstruction cross-entropy summed over pixels,
$\beta=1$ with a six-dimensional latent, Adam with learning rate $10^{-4}$
decayed to $10^{-6}$ in five steps, batch size 32 and weight decay $10^{-5}$.
None of these were tuned, and every arm runs them identically, which is what
allows the results table to be read as an ablation: a difference between arms
cannot be an artefact of optimiser search, because no such search was performed.

Three choices were ours. We train for 100k steps rather than the paper's 240k, a
computational constraint; absolute energy distances consequently sit above
published values, and only the relative ordering of arms trained to an identical
budget is meaningful. We set $\lambda_D=0.01$ and $\lambda_A=10^{-5}$ after
running short 10k-step trials across a range of values, which showed that the
auxiliary terms buy little and cost a great deal: raising $\lambda_A$ to
$10^{-3}$ improved the uncertainty correlation by roughly $0.02$ while Dice fell
from $0.30$ to $0.12$, and $10^{-4}$ became unstable once the \texttt{fcomb} was
corrected, so we kept the smallest weights that still had a measurable effect.
We clip the gradient norm at 100 and clamp the latent log-variance to $\pm10$,
both required for numerical stability as described in Section~\ref{sec:results}.
Augmentation is limited to the random translated crop; the paper additionally
applies elastic deformation, rotation, shearing and scaling. Training draws
$K=4$ samples per step, and evaluation uses 16 samples per image.

\paragraph{Ablation design.} Three arms vary one component at a time.

\paragraph{Tools.} Python, PyTorch, NumPy and Matplotlib. The submitted code runs
in Google Colab as a self-contained notebook that executes the whole pipeline
end-to-end in a reduced ``smoke'' configuration; the same code paths, run at full
length, produced every number reported below.

\section{Results and analysis}\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Metrics.} Dice and IoU, with
$\mathrm{Dice}(A,B)=2|A\cap B|/(|A|+|B|)$, serve as a check that the extension
does not damage segmentation quality; they are computed on the deterministic
prediction against each of the four masks and are not the headline claim. The
generalized energy distance, defined in Section~\ref{sec:intro}, compares our 16
samples with the four human masks. Both it and Dice are evaluated under the
empty-mask convention of the paper's Appendix~B: IoU and Dice are both $0/0$ when
two masks are empty, so the paper defines $d\equiv0$ in that case, and agreement
that no lesion is present is rewarded rather than penalised. We apply the
corresponding rule to Dice and IoU by excluding empty-versus-empty pairs instead
of scoring them zero; without it, the 65\% of crops containing an empty mask
depress every model equally and mask real differences. The correlation
$\mathrm{corr}(U,\Dgt)$ --- whether the model becomes uncertain in the same
places humans disagree --- is the primary result.

Two further diagnostics are needed. Because disagreement in LIDC concentrates on
lesion boundaries, a map that merely marks a boundary already correlates with
$\Dgt$, so $\hat D$ is compared against a trivial control: the 5-pixel boundary
band (dilation minus erosion) of the model's own predicted mask, extending
roughly two pixels inside and two outside its edge. It uses no grader information and is therefore
available at inference, unlike a band drawn around the ground truth. Finally, the subtracted $\mathrm{E}[d(S,S')]$ term of
$D^2_{\mathrm{GED}}$ is reported on its own, since it detects samples that have
collapsed onto a single mask, alongside \emph{nestedness}: the fraction of images
for which, sorting that image's samples by area, each is wholly contained in the
next. Nestedness catches a subtler failure in which the model only inflates and
shrinks one shape without ever proposing a different one, so that its samples can
appear diverse while carrying a single degree of freedom.

\paragraph{Main results.} Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the held-out test split
(1980 images, 16 samples per image), with every arm read from its matched
100k-step checkpoint.

\textbf{Q1 is confirmed.} The head reaches $\mathrm{corr}(\hat D,\Dgt)=0.627$
against its own boundary-band control of $0.425$, a margin of $+0.20$. It has
therefore learned something about annotator behaviour rather than simply tracing
the outline of its own prediction, and the result holds in every arm carrying a
head. This is the clearest positive finding of the project.

\textbf{Q2 is supported but modest.} The correlation $\mathrm{corr}(U,\Dgt)$
rises from $0.483$ to $0.509$ between arms A and C, and arm C simultaneously
attains the best energy distance of the three ELBO arms ($0.388$) at a relative
Dice cost of 4\%. We read the small size of the gain as structural rather than as
a tuning failure. $L_{\text{align}}$ constrains a marginal of a joint object, so
two sample distributions with identical marginals are indistinguishable to it: it
can relocate variance but cannot reshape modes. The effect only became
measurable at all once the latent was able to vary spatially. Our \texttt{fcomb}
initially lacked a nonlinearity between its $1\times1$ convolutions and therefore
collapsed to a single affine map; because the injected $z$ is spatially constant,
every sample became a level set of one function and 100\% of test images had
strictly nested samples, leaving $U$ as a single ring that no loss could reshape.
With the activation applied, nestedness falls to the 1--3\% of
Table~\ref{tab:main}.

\paragraph{Failure cases and challenges.} Our first full-length run suffered
posterior collapse and was discarded. The reconstruction term averaged
cross-entropy over pixels where Kohl et al.\ sum it; at $128\times128$ this
under-weights reconstruction by a factor of about 16000 relative to
$\beta\,\mathrm{KL}$, so $\beta=1$ behaved like $\beta\approx16000$. The KL term
reached zero by step 31k and the validation energy distance \emph{rose} from
$0.34$ to $0.61$ while Dice continued to improve --- the signature of a network
that had become deterministic. Summing the cross-entropy fixed it. With the corrected loss,
gradients are far larger and every arm produced NaNs in the prior encoder within
2k steps, which is why the clipping and clamp of Section~\ref{sec:method} are
required. A related instability appears at $\lambda_A=10^{-4}$, where the KL
doubles at every step until it overflows: $L_{\text{align}}$ back-propagates into
the prior, and inflating the prior variance is the cheapest available way to
raise sample diversity. Detaching the prior in the sampling path removes this
route.

Checkpoint selection also proved misleading. Selecting on best validation energy
distance chooses step 1000 for arm A and step 3000 for arm B, because a
barely-trained model with a wide, uninformative prior scores well on that metric;
we therefore report the matched 100k-step checkpoint for every arm, which
compares methods rather than training durations. Finally, Figure~\ref{fig:qual}
shows a conceptual limit. Much of the disagreement in LIDC concerns lesion
existence, so matching $U$ to $\Dgt$ demands maximal uncertainty across whole
lesions, in direct opposition to the reconstruction term. This bounds how far
$L_{\text{align}}$ can usefully go and motivates the refinement proposed below.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conc}

Where radiologists will disagree is predictable from the image alone: a single
$1\times1$ convolution reaches a correlation of $0.627$ with the true
disagreement map, against $0.425$ for a trivial boundary-band predictor. Aligning
sample diversity with that disagreement produces a smaller but consistent gain,
raising $\mathrm{corr}(U,\Dgt)$ by $0.026$ while attaining the best energy
distance among our ELBO arms at a 4\% Dice cost. The claim we make is not that
our Dice is higher. The baseline already generates diverse plausible
segmentations; our extension makes that diversity correspond to where experts
actually disagree, and turns the disagreement itself into an explicit,
inspectable output. Summarised in one sentence, the design takes its joint
structure from the conditional VAE and its marginal localisation from our loss.

The sharpest limitation is the marginal itself. $\Dgt$ is a per-pixel quantity,
so two graders drawing mask A and two drawing mask B yield exactly the same map
as four graders disagreeing incoherently; neither $\Dgt$ nor $U$ can distinguish
coherent modes from pixel noise, which bounds both the supervision and the
evaluation. Beyond that, we train for 100k of the paper's 240k iterations with
crop-only augmentation, so our absolute energy distances sit above published
values; we study one dataset and one anatomy; $\Dgt$ is estimated from only four
graders; and each arm was run with a single seed, so the Dice gap of arm B cannot
be separated from run-to-run variation.

Two directions follow. Restricting $L_{	ext{align}}$ to a boundary band would
let it shape edge placement without demanding uncertainty across whole lesions,
and is in our view the most likely route to a decisive Q2 result. Comparing
against the Hierarchical Probabilistic U-Net on the same split would place these
numbers in context.
